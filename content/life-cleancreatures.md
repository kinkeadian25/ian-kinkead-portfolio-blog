The creatures in the glass rooms liked things smooth. They had four limbs, soft skin, and a habit of acting as if friction were an insult.

Their desks had no wires showing. Their air stayed at seventy-one degrees all year without anybody opening a window. They used words like cloud and alignment and framework until the whole floor sounded like software with no moving parts. It was a very polite arrangement, the way a funeral parlor is polite to a body: everything combed, nothing leaking, everybody whispering like quiet were a virtue, no complex signs of human life. Perfect.

Two floors down, behind a steel door with a yellow sticker, the building had a stomach that growled all night so the throat upstairs could keep humming. Big iron was pulling heat out of the glass rooms, shoving air through fat metal tubes, and burning fuel sucked out of the ground three states away. Nobody from upstairs ever went past the yellow sticker. To look in there was to admit the quiet had a price, and the quiet was the whole point.

Eventually, though, somebody has to look in there. A seal blows, a pipe sweats, a breaker trips, and the neat seventy-one-degree spell comes apart in minutes. A tech in stained coveralls goes in with a flashlight and a wrench, fixes what he can, and leaves the smell behind the steel door.

From a distance, it is hard to see why they stop at the basement. Once a species learns how good clean air feels, it starts treating geography like a bigger set of ducts. Far off, in places built mostly around cooking, stamping, and smelting, the sky stays thick and the ditches run odd colors so glass towers somewhere else can keep their reflections clear, boxes arrive wrapped in crisp paper, and nobody has to remember what went into the glue.

Nothing ever really disappears. It just gets pushed behind another door, or past another line on a map, until the whole world is just clean lobbies stacked on top of dark rooms nobody wants to visit. I find it humorous most humans believe they will end up in a lobby.

On the third floor, a woman clicked to a slide that said Zero-Emission Futures.

Everybody felt very clean.
